Buffer action — same NaOH, different pH change

Add 5.0 mL of 4.0 M NaOH to 1 L of pure water and to 1 L of an acetic acid buffer solution, side by side. The pH of both beakers is calculated by solving the charge-balance equation exactly. Dilute the buffer components or add more base, and find for yourself the moment when the buffer capacity breaks down.

Learning objectives After this activity you can …
explain, from the reactions of the components, the difference in pH change when the same OH⁻ is added to a buffer and to water.
calculate the pH of a buffer after adding a strong base in two steps: stoichiometry → equilibrium.
show by experiment how the amount of buffer components relates to the buffer capacity.
  • Key summary 1 and 3 — shown after you submit your prediction.
  • Key summary 1 — A strong base (OH⁻) reacts completely with the weak acid HA of the buffer and turns it into A⁻.
  • Key summary 2 — shown after you submit your prediction.
  • Key summary 2 — The pH depends only on the log of the ratio [A⁻]/[HA], so the pH change is small (4.74 → 4.80).
  • Key summary 3 — When HA runs out, the leftover OH⁻ sets the pH directly, just as in water (the buffer breaks down).

Key terms

Buffer solution: a solution that contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) in similar amounts, so its pH changes little when a small amount of strong acid or strong base is added. · Conjugate acid–base pair: HA/A⁻, which differ by one H⁺ (here CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻). · Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). · Buffer capacity: the amount of strong acid or strong base a buffer can take up before its pH changes greatly. · Charge balance: total positive charge = total negative charge in the solution.

Explanation notes (open after prediction)

The two-step calculation (stoichiometry → equilibrium) and the explanation of the pH changes in water and in the buffer open after you submit your prediction. For now, think about what the added OH⁻ meets in each beaker.

Two-step calculation

① Stoichiometry: OH⁻ reacts completely with HA (K = 1/Kb ≈ 10⁹, practically complete). 0.020 mol OH⁻ → HA 0.300→0.280 mol, A⁻ 0.300→0.320 mol. ② Equilibrium: use the remaining HA/A⁻ to find the pH. The volume (1.005 L) cancels in the ratio.

pH = 4.74 + log(0.320 / 0.280) = 4.80

Why water changes so much · buffer breakdown

Water has no HA to take up the OH⁻, so [OH⁻] = 0.020/1.005 = 0.0199 M → pOH 1.70 → pH 12.30. A buffer also jumps in pH once its HA is completely used up: the leftover OH⁻ then sets the pH (buffer capacity exceeded). Soils resist pH change in a similar way: a soil with a larger buffer capacity needs more lime to change its pH.

[Na⁺] + [H⁺] = [A⁻] + [OH⁻] (+ [Cl⁻])
Limits of this model — activity coefficients ignored (ideal solution), 25 °C · Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴, Ka(CH₃COOH) = 1.8×10⁻⁵, volumes are additive, CO₂ uptake from air ignored. The starting pH 7.00 of "pure water" is an ideal value; real distilled water is about 5.6 because of CO₂. The pH on screen is a numerical solution of the charge balance, without approximation (x is not neglected).

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

Demonstration in 3 steps

  • ① Standard condition (0.300 M, NaOH 5.0 mL): buffer ΔpH 0.06 vs water ΔpH 5.30.
  • ② In the bar chart, HA↓ and A⁻↑ — "Where did the OH⁻ go?"
  • ③ Lower the concentration to 0.020 M, or add 75 mL of NaOH or more, to show the buffer breakdown.

Three key questions

  • "The amount of OH⁻ is the same. Why does only one beaker reach pH 12?"
  • "Why did the buffer pH change at all, even by 0.06?"
  • "Can a buffer be used forever? What sets the limit?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-buf-01: "it rises as much as water" → use the bars to show that OH⁻ is used up by HA.
  • MC-buf-02: "it does not change at all" → the ratio 0.320/0.280 is not 1, so the pH rises by 0.06.
  • MC-buf-03: "capacity is unlimited" → HA 0.300 mol = NaOH 75 mL is the limit.
