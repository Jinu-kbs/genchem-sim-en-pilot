Key summary Three key ideas
  1. The solubility-product constant Ksp is fixed at a given temperature, but the molar solubility can change with other equilibria in the solution.
  2. The ionization constant of a weak acid HA is Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA].An anion that is the conjugate base of a weak acid combines with H⁺ to form a weak-acid molecule, so the [H⁺] of the solution sets the free concentration of that anion.
  3. To judge the molar solubility, first check whether the ions formed when the salt dissolves take part in other equilibria in the solution.Anions that are conjugate bases of strong acids (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO₃⁻) hardly combine with H⁺ in water.

Key terms

Molar solubility s: moles of salt that dissolve in 1 L of saturated solution (mol/L). · Solubility-product constant Ksp: the equilibrium constant between the solid and its saturated solution (the solid is not written in the expression). · Conjugate base: the species left after an acid gives away H⁺ (HF → F⁻). · Fraction α(F⁻) = [F⁻] / ([F⁻] + [HF]) = Ka / (Ka + [H⁺]). · Buffered (fixed pH): a solution whose pH hardly changes even when acid is used up.

The molar solubility is found without approximation by putting the mass balance 2[Pb²⁺] = [F⁻] + [HF] and the Ka expression into the Ksp expression. The pH is assumed to be held by a buffer (why this is needed is explained in "Limits" after the prediction).

Why PbF₂ dissolves better in acid

F⁻ is the conjugate base of the weak acid HF, so it combines with H⁺ to form HF. When free [F⁻] drops, Q = [Pb²⁺][F⁻]² becomes smaller than Ksp and more solid dissolves. H⁺ reacts with F⁻, not with Pb²⁺. Overall reaction: PbF₂(s) + 2H⁺ ⇌ Pb²⁺ + 2HF.

Ksp = s · (2s·α)² = 4s³α² , α = Ka / (Ka + [H⁺])

PbI₂ and AgCl do not depend on pH

I⁻ and Cl⁻ are conjugate bases of strong acids (HI, HCl), so they hardly combine with H⁺ (α = 1). Adding acid leaves the free anion concentration unchanged, and so the molar solubility is unchanged. The more basic the anion (the smaller the Ka of its conjugate acid), the larger the effect of pH — check this with the Ka slider. In soil science this is why the solubility of a heavy-metal salt in acidic soil depends on its anion, not only on the metal.

If [H⁺] ≫ Ka, s ∝ [H⁺]^(2/3)
Limits Limits of this model

The model uses 25 °C, activity coefficients of 1 (ideal solution) and a pH held by a buffer. At pH 1, the HF formed as PbF₂ dissolves uses up about 0.12 M of H⁺, so 0.1 M strong acid alone cannot keep the pH at 1 (this is why a buffer must be assumed). Ion pairs such as PbF⁺, hydrolysis of Pb²⁺ and activity effects in concentrated solutions (s > 0.1 M) are not included — values in that range are model values. Ksp values are from Martell and Smith, Critical Stability Constants (PbF₂ 3.6×10⁻⁸ = reference value for this topic, CaF₂ 3.9×10⁻¹¹, AgCl 1.8×10⁻¹⁰). Values that differ by more than 5% from the CRC Handbook are listed in the teacher layer. For PbI₂ the two sources differ by 24%, so its value is shown but not graded.

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explain and Elaborate, 15–20 min. After the common-ion effect, before complex-ion formation. Whole-class demonstration (?mode=teacher) or individual work.

Demonstration in 3 steps

  • ① PbF₂ at pH 7 → s 2.1×10⁻³ M. ② pH 1 (buffered) → 5.8×10⁻² M, about 28 times. ③ Switch to PbI₂ and repeat → a flat line.

Three key questions

  • "Does H⁺ react with Pb²⁺ or with F⁻?"
  • "Ksp stays the same. How can the molar solubility increase?"
  • "Why is a buffer needed to keep the pH at 1?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-pHsol-01 "acid dissolves every salt better" → ignores how basic the anion is. PbI₂ and AgCl do not depend on pH.
  • MC-pHsol-02 "H⁺ reacts with the metal ion" → ions of the same charge do not combine. Correct it with F⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ HF.

Calculation method (E2 exact equations)

  • At fixed pH, combining Ksp = [M²⁺][F⁻]², [HF] = [H⁺][F⁻]/Ka and 2[M²⁺] = [F⁻] + [HF] gives the exact closed form Ksp = 4s³α² (no approximation). For AgCl s = √Ksp; for PbI₂ s = (Ksp/4)^(1/3).
  • Buffer assumption: at pH 1 the HF formed, 2s(1 − α) ≈ 0.116 M, uses up H⁺, so 0.1 M HCl alone cannot keep the pH. Students see this in the "H⁺ used up" line on screen.
