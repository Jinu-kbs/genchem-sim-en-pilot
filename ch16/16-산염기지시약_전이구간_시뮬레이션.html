Entry check Before you start — prerequisites and key terms

What is an acid–base indicator?

A weak-acid (or weak-base) dye whose acid form and base form have different colors A substance that reacts and forms a color only when an acid is present A substance that brings a solution to neutral (pH 7)
Key terms

Indicator (HIn): a weak-acid dye whose acid form HIn and base form In⁻ have different colors. Equilibrium: HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. · Transition range: the pH range where both forms are present and the color changes gradually (usually about 2 pH units). · Possible pH range: all pH values that agree with the observed color. · pH: −log[H⁺]. At 25 °C, below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic.

Why the color changes only inside a "range"

An indicator is a weak acid, HIn. In HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻, HIn (acid-form color) dominates at low pH and In⁻ (base-form color) dominates at high pH. Our eyes see the color of one form when that form is about 10 times more abundant than the other. So the color changes only within about pKa ± 1, that is, within about 2 pH units. Outside this range, the color stays the same however much the pH changes. Example: methyl red is red at pH 4.5 and below, turns gradually yellow from 4.5 to 6.0, and is yellow at 6.0 and above.

One color = one pH range

Colorless phenolphthalein only means "pH is below about 8.2". A solution at pH 3 (acidic), pH 7 (neutral) or pH 7.8 (weakly basic) is colorless in every case. So colorless ≠ acidic. To narrow the range, use indicators with different transition ranges together. This is why pH paper is coated with a mixture of several indicators. Soil pH test kits use the same idea.

Limits of this model

  • The transition-range limits are approximate values (±0.3) taken from standard literature tables. They change with temperature, ionic strength, indicator concentration and the observer.
  • The screen colors are approximations and may differ from real colors. Base your decision on the color names written as text.
  • The limits are not sharp lines. The model simplifies "outside the range = color of one form". Do not read results such as a 0.1-unit gap between two indicator limits as real precision. Phenolphthalein slowly becomes colorless again in very strong base (pH above about 13); this model does not include that.

Key summary

  • An indicator changes color only inside its own transition range of about 2 pH units.
  • What an observed color tells you (a value or a range?) is shown after you submit your prediction.An observed color gives a pH range, not a pH value (colorless phenolphthalein → pH < about 8.2).
  • Combining indicators with different transition ranges narrows the possible range (the overlap = the intersection).

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explore and Explain, 15 min. Use before a lab (indicator colors, neutralization) as preparation, or after the lab as a summary.

Demonstration in 3 steps

  • ① Add only phenolphthalein and move pH 2 → 7 → 7.8 → 9 → 11. Stress that it is still colorless at 7.8.
  • ② Add methyl red + bromthymol blue + phenolphthalein together and compare pH 5, 7 and 9.
  • ③ Switch to unknown sample mode. Let students discuss the order of indicators, then use "Reveal unknown pH" in the teacher bar.

Three key questions

  • "Which pH values does the observation 'colorless' rule out, and which does it not rule out?"
  • "Why does each indicator change color at a different pH, not at pH 7?" (their pKa values differ)
  • "Why does pH paper contain a mixture of several indicators?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-ind-01: "colorless phenolphthalein = acidic" → colorless means pH < about 8.2, which includes neutral and weakly basic solutions.
  • MC-ind-02: "all indicators change at pH 7" → methyl violet 0–1.6, alizarin yellow R 10.1–12.0, and so on: each is different.
  • MC-ind-03: "one indicator gives the exact pH" → one indicator gives only a range (use a pH meter for precise measurement).
  • Note on photographs: published photos of salt solutions with indicators may show colors that do not match the range definitions (for example, solutions inside a transition range shown as pure red or yellow). Photo colors are approximate; this simulation follows the range definitions.
