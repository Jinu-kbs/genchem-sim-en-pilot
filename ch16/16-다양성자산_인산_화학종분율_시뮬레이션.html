An acid such as phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) can give away more than one proton. Which species dominates at each pH? Key summary Three key ideas
  1. A polyprotic acid gives away its protons one at a time, step by step. Each step has its own acid dissociation constant (Ka1, Ka2, Ka3).
  2. The species fraction α is the share of one species in the total acid concentration. The fractions of all species add up to 1.
  3. When the pH changes, the position of each step's equilibrium changes, and so do the ratios between the species.In each step, the concentration ratio of conjugate base to conjugate acid is set by the ratio of that step's Ka to [H⁺] (Ka = [H⁺][conjugate base]/[conjugate acid]).

Key terms

Stepwise ionization: H₃PO₄ ⇌ H⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻ (Ka1), H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ (Ka2), HPO₄²⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ (Ka3). · pKa = −log Ka. · Species fraction α: concentration of one species ÷ total acid concentration (0 to 1). · Dominant species: the species with the largest fraction at that pH. · Charge balance: total positive charge concentration = total negative charge concentration.

Fractions are calculated exactly: α = (term of that species)/(h³ + Ka1h² + Ka1Ka2h + Ka1Ka2Ka3), with h = [H⁺]. "The pH of this solution" is found by solving the charge balance [H⁺] = [OH⁻] + Σ(charge × concentration) without approximation, for the acid alone dissolved at C M.

At pH = pKa, two neighboring species are half and half

Take the log of Ka2 = [H⁺][HPO₄²⁻]/[H₂PO₄⁻]: pKa2 = pH + log([H₂PO₄⁻]/[HPO₄²⁻]). When pH equals pKa2 (7.21), the log term is 0, so the two species have equal concentrations — two distribution curves cross near 50%. When pH is 1 unit above pKa, there is about 10 times more conjugate base; 2 units above, about 100 times more.

pH = pKa ⟹ [conjugate acid] = [conjugate base]

pH 7.21 is more than 5 units below pKa3 (12.38), so there is almost no PO₄³⁻ (about 10⁻⁵). The idea "neutral means all protons are lost" is wrong. In soil water near pH 5–8, the same curves show that phosphate from fertilizer exists mainly as H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻.

The pH of a polyprotic acid solution is set almost entirely by the first step

When Ka1 and Ka2 differ by a factor of 10³ or more, the H⁺ from the second step is negligible. For example, for 0.0037 M H₂CO₃, Ka1 alone gives [H⁺] = 4.0×10⁻⁵ M, pH 4.40, and [CO₃²⁻] is only 5.6×10⁻¹¹ M, almost equal to Ka2. The "solution pH" in this simulation checks this with the exact charge-balance solution that includes every step and water.

[H⁺] = [OH⁻] + [H₂A⁻] + 2[HA²⁻] + 3[A³⁻]
Limits Limits of this model

The model uses 25 °C, Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ and activity coefficients of 1 (ideal solution). The fraction curves assume the pH is held by a buffer. Ka values are common reference values for polyprotic acids. Some sources label pKa3 as 12.67, but Ka3 = 4.2×10⁻¹³ gives 12.38, so this simulation uses the Ka values as the reference. Constants that differ by more than 5% from literature values (CRC Handbook, 84th ed. · Wikipedia) are listed in the teacher layer. For carbonic acid, Ka1 is the apparent value that counts all dissolved CO₂ as H₂CO₃.

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explore and Explain, 20–25 min. Right after monoprotic weak acids, before buffer solutions. Whole-class demonstration (?mode=teacher) or individual work.

Demonstration in 3 steps

  • ① Phosphoric acid, scan pH automatically from 0 to 14 — the dominant species changes four times.
  • ② Stop at pH 7.21 — two bars are almost the same length; the third anion is near 0.
  • ③ Carbonic acid 0.0037 M, "Go to the pH of this solution" — 4.40; the second step contributes almost nothing.

Three key questions

  • "Can you predict from the table where two curves cross?"
  • "The acid can give three H⁺. Why is [H⁺] of the acid solution not three times larger?"
  • "HPO₄²⁻ dominates over the wide range between pKa2 and pKa3. What does this mean?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-poly-01 "mostly PO₄³⁻ at pH 7.21" → the belief that neutral = fully deprotonated. pH is 5 below pKa3, so the PO₄³⁻ fraction is about 10⁻⁵.
  • MC-poly-02 "a diprotic acid doubles [H⁺]" → Ka2 is nearly 10⁴ times smaller than Ka1, so H⁺ from the second step is about 10⁻⁶ of the first. Counting it twice gives 4.10 instead of 4.40.

Calculation method (E2 exact equations)

  • Fractions: αᵢ = (h^(n−i)·K1…Kᵢ)/Σ, no approximation. Solution pH: f(h) = h − Kw/h − C·Σ i·αᵢ(h) = 0 solved by bisection in log space (f increases monotonically with h).

Reference values and a labeling inconsistency

  • Phosphoric acid 7.5×10⁻³ / 6.2×10⁻⁸ / 4.2×10⁻¹³ → pKa 2.12 / 7.21 / 12.38. Carbonic acid 4.3×10⁻⁷ / 5.6×10⁻¹¹, oxalic acid 5.9×10⁻² / 6.4×10⁻⁵, citric acid 7.4×10⁻⁴ / 1.7×10⁻⁵ / 4.0×10⁻⁷.
  • Labels pKa1 2.12 and pKa2 7.21 agree with the Ka table, but a pKa3 label of 12.67 seen in some graphs differs from 12.38 calculated from Ka3. Reference = the Ka table.
  • 0.0037 M H₂CO₃: pH 4.40, [CO₃²⁻] = 5.6×10⁻¹¹ M — exact solution 4.4015.

Literature check (2 sources: LibreTexts Table E1 = CRC Handbook 84th ed. · Wikipedia page of each substance, checked 2026-10-03)
