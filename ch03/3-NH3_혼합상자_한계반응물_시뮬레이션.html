The number of ammonia molecules formed is not an input. It follows from molecules pairing up in the coefficient (mole) ratio. First predict, then change the molecule numbers and observe, and finally check yourself.

Key summary: (1) The coefficients in N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ give the ratio in which molecules react (1:3:2). (2) When one reactant runs out, the reaction stops and the other reactant is left over. (3) Set the numbers in the box, press [Run reaction], and see which reactant runs out first.

Key terms

Mole: a counting unit for particles (here we compare numbers of molecules directly). · Coefficient ratio (mole ratio): the ratio of the coefficients in the equation, N₂:H₂:NH₃ = 1:3:2. · Batch: how many complete reaction sets a reactant can supply (number ÷ its coefficient). · Limiting reactant: the reactant that runs out first relative to the coefficient ratio and stops the reaction. · Excess reactant: the reactant that is not used up and is left over.

Key idea 1 · Limiting reactant = the one that is short relative to the coefficient ratio

The limiting reactant is not simply the one with fewer molecules. Compare the reactants after dividing by the coefficients (N₂:H₂ = 1:3). Example: with N₂ = 2 and H₂ = 3, N₂ gives 2 batches and H₂ gives 3 ÷ 3 = 1 batch. H₂ gives fewer batches, so H₂ is the limiting reactant and N₂ is left over.

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Batches: N₂ batches = N₂ | H₂ batches = H₂ ÷ 3 → the smaller one is limiting

Key idea 2 · NH₃ formed and leftover

N₂ that reacts = min(N₂, H₂÷3 rounded down). One N₂ gives two NH₃, so NH₃ formed = 2 × (N₂ that reacted). After the reaction, the limiting reactant is 0 and the other reactant is left over (excess). The same idea is used for nitrogen fertilizer: ammonia made from N₂ and H₂ is the starting point of most nitrogen fertilizers.

NH₃ = 2 × min(N₂, floor(H₂/3))
Limiting reactant used up to 0 | excess reactant left over

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explore · Explain stage. 10–15 min after the introduction.
  • Works as a whole-class demo (projector, ?mode=teacher) or as individual work on one device per student.

Demo in 3 steps

  • (1) N₂=2, H₂=6 → both used up, NH₃=4 (exact 1:3 mole ratio).
  • (2) N₂=2, H₂=3 → H₂ limiting, NH₃=2, 1 N₂ left over (N₂ has fewer molecules but is NOT limiting).
  • (3) N₂=3, H₂=12 → N₂ limiting, NH₃=6, 3 H₂ left over.

3 key questions

  • "There are fewer N₂ molecules. So why does H₂ run out first?"
  • "Which reactant decides the number of NH₃ molecules formed?"
  • "The reaction has stopped. Why is one kind of molecule still there?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-lim-01: "Limiting reactant = the one with fewer molecules" → it is the one with fewer batches after dividing by the coefficient. With N₂=2, H₂=3, H₂ is limiting.
  • MC-lim-02: "Product formed = amount of limiting reactant" → use the coefficient ratio. NH₃ formed = 2 × (N₂ that reacted).

Answer key (by level, Q2 · Q3 · Q4)

  • Basic: Q2 4 NH₃ (e.g. N₂ 2 · H₂ 6) · Q3 N₂ 2 · H₂ 9 → 4 (N₂ limiting) · Q4 limiting reactant
  • Standard: Q2 H₂ limiting · 6 NH₃ (e.g. N₂ 4 · H₂ 9) · Q3 N₂ 3 · H₂ 12 → 6 (N₂ limiting) · Q4 limiting reactant
  • Advanced: Q2 H₂ limiting · 1 N₂ left · 8 NH₃ (N₂ 5 · H₂ 12–14) · Q3 N₂ 5 · H₂ 12 → 8 (H₂ limiting; the "fewer molecules" misconception gives 10) · Q4 excess reactant
  • Q5 (apply): H₂ limiting, 3 N₂ react, 1 N₂ left over.

Prerequisites

  • Coefficients in a chemical equation and balancing; the mole and mole ratio.
