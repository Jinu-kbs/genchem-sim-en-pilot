Precipitation Reaction —
Ions Combining

When you mix two solutions, their ions meet in one beaker. What happens depends on which ion pairs meet. The amount formed is not an input: it emerges from the particles meeting. First predict, then run it and observe, and finally check yourself.

Key summary: (1) In a solution of an electrolyte, the ions are separated into cations and anions that move freely. (2) When two solutions are mixed, new ion pairs meet, and different pairs can behave differently. (3) The number of precipitate particles is not set by you; it is counted from the pairs that bind.

Key terms

Electrolyte · ionization: a substance that dissolves in water and separates into ions. · Spectator ion: an ion that does not take part in the reaction and keeps moving freely. · Precipitate: an insoluble solid that forms when a pair with very low solubility binds and settles out.

Precipitation reaction · net ionic equation

Ions in solution are spread out and move freely. When two solutions are mixed and a pair with very low solubility meets, the ions bind and settle as a solid precipitate. Ions that do not take part and keep moving are called spectator ions. The same kind of reaction happens in soil and irrigation water, for example when calcium ions meet phosphate from fertilizer.

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)↓
Net ionic equation · Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators (they cancel on both sides)

Emergence · the amount of precipitate comes from the number of bindings

We never set the amount of precipitate. Freely moving Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ come close and bind; the number of these bindings adds up to the number of precipitate particles. With only spectators (Na⁺·NO₃⁻) there is no insoluble pair, so the precipitate is 0.

Precipitate count = Σ (bindings of insoluble pairs)
Reacting pair → precipitate grows | spectators only → precipitate 0

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explore · Explain stage. 10–15 min after the introduction.
  • Works as a whole-class demo (projector, ?mode=teacher) or as individual work on one device per student.

Demo in 3 steps

  • (1) Spectators only (Na⁺·NO₃⁻) → precipitate 0 (free motion only).
  • (2) Reacting pair ON (Ag⁺·Cl⁻) → observe binding and growing precipitate.
  • (3) Both pairs ON → only Ag⁺·Cl⁻ precipitate; Na⁺·NO₃⁻ stay spectators to the end.

Answer key (by level)

  • Prediction: a solid precipitate forms. Q1: (b). Q4 blank: precipitate.
  • Basic: Q2 reacting pair only, precipitate ≥ 6 · Q3 0 / Standard: Q2 both pairs on, ≥ 8 · Q3 min(10, 6) = 6 / Advanced: Q2 both pairs on, ≥ 12 · Q3 min(12, 9) = 9
  • Q2 counts only if the student changes the controls after predicting. Common Q3 errors: adding spectators or leftover ions, or giving the larger ion count.

3 key questions

  • "We never set the number of precipitate particles, yet a number appears. Where does it come from?"
  • "Why do Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ bind, but Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ do not?"
  • "Does mixing two solutions always give a precipitate?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-precip-01: "Mixing always gives a precipitate" → only an insoluble pair precipitates (NaNO₃ is soluble).
  • MC-precip-02: "Spectator ions also react" → they are onlookers that cancel on both sides and do not appear in the net ionic equation.

Prerequisites

  • Electrolytes and ionization (ions in aqueous solution); solubility rules (soluble / insoluble salts).
