Gas Solubility and Pressure —
Henry's Law Sg = kPg

The concentration of dissolved gas is not an input. It settles by itself from the balance of particles going in and coming out at the surface. First predict, then push the piston and observe, and finally check yourself.

Estimated time 15–20 min · Prerequisites: partial pressure, molarity, dynamic equilibrium

Key summary

1. Gas molecules keep entering the liquid at the surface (dissolving) and leaving it (escaping). Equilibrium is reached when the two rates are equal.
2. The concentration of dissolved gas at equilibrium is the solubility Sg under those conditions. The gas side is described by the partial pressure Pg.
3. The Henry's law constant k links Sg and Pg. In the activity you find out yourself which change alters k.3. The Henry's law constant k is the proportionality constant between Sg and Pg. It depends on the kind of gas, the solvent and the temperature.

The result summary (what happens to Sg and k when the pressure is doubled) opens after you lock in a prediction.

Result: Sg = kPg — when Pg doubles, S_g also doubles at the new equilibrium, and k (the slope) stays the same. When the temperature rises, k for a gas becomes smaller.

Key terms

Partial pressure Pg: the pressure exerted by one gas in a gas mixture. · Solubility Sg: the concentration of dissolved gas at equilibrium (mM = mmol/L). · Henry's law constant k: the constant in Sg = kPg; it depends on the solute, the solvent and the temperature. · Dynamic equilibrium: rate of dissolving = rate of escaping.

Pressure ↑ → solubility ↑

At equilibrium, gas molecules enter the solution as fast as they leave it. When the piston halves the volume, the gas pressure doubles and more molecules hit the surface (going in). The solubility then rises until the dissolved concentration is high enough for the escape rate to match again. The same law applies to O₂ and CO₂ dissolved in paddy-field water and in nutrient solutions for hydroponics.

Sg = k Pg
Henry's law · k depends on the solute, the solvent and the temperature

A straight line through the origin

A plot of solubility against partial pressure is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is k. In water at 25 ℃ the slopes are in the order O₂ > CO > N₂ > He. Example: N₂ at 79.03 kPa gives 4.75×10⁻⁴ M, k = 6.1×10⁻⁶ mol/m³·Pa.

k = Sg / Pg
Changing the pressure does not change the slope (k) · temperature ↑ → k of a gas ↓
Limits Limits of the model

This is a 2D particle model. In real water, the dissolved O₂ is only a few % (or less) of the molecules in the gas phase, so this model ignores depletion of the gas phase and draws the dissolved particles on a different scale (1 particle = a fixed number of mM). The mM value is the measured number of dissolved particles × the scale; the exact value k(T)·Pg is calculated from literature constants. The reaction of CO₂ with water (forming H₂CO₃) and deviations from Henry's law at high pressure or in concentrated solutions are not included.

Teacher notes — open the lesson plan

Lesson plan · 5E phase and time

  • Explore · Explain. 15–20 min. Whole-class demo (?mode=teacher) or one device per student.

Demo in 3 steps

  • (1) O₂, 25 ℃, V=100%: equilibrium with similar numbers of dissolve and escape arrows, S ≈ 1.32 mM (k·101.3 kPa).
  • (2) V=50%: P doubles at once to 202.7 kPa → transient state (dissolving > escaping) → after about 5 s, S ≈ 2.63 mM (2 times), S/P unchanged.
  • (3) Raise the temperature to 40 ℃ or switch to He → the slope of the line (k) changes. Temperature and the kind of gas change k; pressure does not.

3 key questions

  • "The moment you push the piston, does S double at once? What changes first?"
  • "When you doubled the pressure, the point on the graph moved. Did the line move?"
  • "Why does a warm soft drink go flat faster?"

Common misconceptions

  • MC-sol-01: "Doubling the pressure doubles the Henry's law constant k" → k is the slope; the pressure is the position on the line. Changing P only moves the point along the line.
  • MC-sol-02: "Solubility is a fixed property of the substance and does not depend on pressure" → roughly true for solids and liquids, but gas solubility is directly proportional to the partial pressure.

Model description

  • 150 gas particles (fixed) hit the surface and form dissolved particles with probability p_in ∝ k(T)·T; dissolved particles escape at the surface with probability 0.5. The equilibrium number of dissolved particles emerges in proportion to the collision frequency (∝ P). In external checks (long headless averages) it agrees with the exact value kP within ±3% on average, and a single snapshot agrees within about ±5% (mission tolerance: Basic 20%, Standard 15%, Advanced 10%). Revision r2 (2026-10-03): the mission is not judged from a snapshot. After compression, 1200 steps are discarded; the mission succeeds when the whole 95% confidence interval of the mean of 8–10 blocks of 600 steps lies within kP ± tolerance (E1 confidence-interval test, false-negative test with 200 seeds).

Note on the N₂ constant

  • The often-quoted relation "(4.75×10⁻⁴ mol/L)/79.03 kPa = 6.1×10⁻⁶ mol/m³·Pa" is slightly off: 0.475 mol/m³ ÷ 79,030 Pa = 6.0×10⁻⁶ (6.01×10⁻⁶). It probably comes from converting 6.1×10⁻⁴ M/atm. The simulation uses 6.1×10⁻⁶, so 79.03 kPa gives 0.482 mM (1.5% above 0.475 mM). A student who uses 0.475 is still within the tolerance for Basic Q3.
  • The literature N₂ value is 6.4×10⁻⁶ (henrys-law.org).
